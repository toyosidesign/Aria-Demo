Use case · Aria

From a PDF brief to a plan I can start today

Students do not miss deadlines because they cannot write. They miss them because they start four days too late. The brief already holds everything a plan needs, and every tool asks them to type it in again.

User
A second year law student with four assignments open and no idea which to start.
Trigger
The brief lands in the module portal, usually as a PDF.
Outcome
A dated plan working back from the deadline, first step pinned, submission time reserved before any work is placed.
My role
Problem framing, flow design, product copy. Shipped the flow and the test suite that holds it.
Status
Shipped
01

Empathize

The plan is the work nobody does

What did I learn about the person before designing anything?

Watching how students actually handle a new assignment, the pattern was the same every time. The brief gets read once, the deadline goes in a calendar, and nothing else happens for a fortnight. The document holds the deliverable, the deadline, the weighting and the marking criteria. All of it is retyped later, badly, from memory.

The assumption I lost early: that people want to be taught how to plan. They do not. They want the plan, and they want it to already exist.

Evidence

Briefs collected from three modules, the workaround recorded end to end: portal, PDF, calendar entry, nothing.
02

Define

A deadline says when to finish, and nothing says when to begin

What is the problem, stated so it can be solved or failed?

Point of view. A student needs to know when to start, because every tool they own tells them when to finish.

How might we turn a document they already have into a plan they did not have to build?

  • Success: the plan states a start date, and reserves submission time as a visible row.
  • Success: a fact Aria is unsure of is labelled, not presented as certain.
  • Out of scope: writing the essay. That is a different product and a worse one.
03

Ideate

Three ways in, and the one the constraint chose

What else could this have been?

  • A form. Fields for deliverable, deadline, weighting, criteria. Rejected: it is transcription, and transcription is the thing being complained about.
  • A conversation. Aria asks the same questions one at a time. Rejected here: slower than the form for facts that are already written down, though it is the right shape for an event, where nothing is written down.
  • Read the document. Chosen. The student already did the work of obtaining it.

The constraint decided it. A PDF cannot be read on the device, so the file has to be sent to a model, and a model can be wrong. That single fact turned confidence into a requirement rather than a nicety: every extracted fact carries how sure Aria is, and a missing fact is never shown as a blank.

04

Prototype

Upload, read back, plan backwards

What was actually built?

  • Upload the brief. The primary action, because it is the fastest true path. Filling it in yourself stays available and never becomes the default.
  • Read it back with confidence. Deliverable, deadline, weighting, marking criteria, format rules. Each one labelled clear, probably, or worth checking.
  • Gaps become buttons. A missing fact renders as three ways forward: ask the tutor, upload the handbook, I know this.
  • Commitments. The calendar is read, and the standing weekly ones are asked once.
  • The plan. Built backwards from the deadline, days shared out by the marking criteria, submission buffer reserved first.
The decision a careful person would question. Why plan backwards at all? Because forwards planning answers "what shall I do first", which is not the question a deadline asks. Working back is the only direction that produces a start date, and the start date is the entire value.
05

Test

Arithmetic that decides when somebody starts working

How do I know it works?

This maths decides the day a student begins, so it is asserted rather than eyeballed. The cases covered are the ones nobody wants to reproduce by hand on a phone.

  • deadline tomorrow
  • deadline already gone
  • more steps than days
  • every useful day taken
  • no steps at all
  • buffer vs. a two day window

Testing caught a real defect before anyone met it: a date calculation that shortened the planning horizon by one day for every user east of Greenwich. It now runs in three timezones on every change.

What I would measure in production

Days between plan accepted and first step completed. Share of plans where the buffer survives to the deadline. Share of briefs where a fact was corrected by the student, which is the honest measure of whether the confidence labels are calibrated.
Still unproven. Whether students start on the day the plan says. A demo build has no production cohort, and inventing a number would be worse than admitting that.

Constraints and trade-offs

What I gave up, and what I chose not to claim

  • Reading a brief costs money per document. So extraction is capped, never repeated over an answer that already exists, and the upload is one request rather than a conversation with a file attached to every turn.
  • A model can be wrong about a deadline. Confidence is shown on every fact, and a date it cannot resolve is left empty rather than guessed. An invented deadline is the single worst thing this product could do.
  • When Aria has nothing to go on, it says so. With only a title, the guide asks for the one thing that would help instead of generating four directions that would fit any essay ever written.

The same template, applied elsewhere in this product

The daily review and where the paid tier line falls. The guide for a student who is stuck. Swipe to complete, which was two bugs wearing one costume. The project definition of done gate. Onboarding's opening question, which used to decide the answer inside the question.

Aria is a demo build: an assistant for students that captures work in conversation, plans it against the deadline, and offers to do it. Written as a use case, not as a release note.
